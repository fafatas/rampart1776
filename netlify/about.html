About Rampart 1776

The conversation started in 1776.
It was never meant to end.

Rampart exists because the republic the Founders built doesn't run itself — and because the principles they fought for are worth understanding, defending, and passing on.

Where this began

It wasn't just the Founders.

History remembers Washington, Madison, Jefferson, and Hamilton. It tends to forget the farmer who left his field, the printer's apprentice who set the type, the tavern keeper who fed the army on credit he'd never collect, and the wife who kept the farm running while her husband was gone for years at a time.

The tavern was where the revolution was argued into existence. Common people — not statesmen — reading pamphlets aloud, debating principles over ale, deciding what they believed and whether it was worth dying for. The Federalist Papers were newspaper columns. Common Sense was written deliberately in plain language so the person in the tavern could read it, understand it, and make up their own mind. The Founders weren't writing for archives. They were writing for the room.

The Minutemen weren't professional soldiers. They were farmers, tradesmen, and shopkeepers who set down their tools and stood up for principles they understood and believed in. They read the pamphlets. They argued in the taverns. They debated after church. They knew what they were fighting for — not in the abstract, but in the specific, detailed, principled way that only comes from actually engaging with the ideas.

That engagement — ordinary people wrestling seriously with serious ideas — is what made the American republic possible. And it's what keeps it alive. Every generation has to do it again.

"Knowledge will forever govern ignorance; and a people who mean to be their own governors must arm themselves with the power which knowledge gives."
— James Madison, letter to W.T. Barry, 1822

A note from the founder

I built Rampart because I believe the principles of America's founding are worth fighting for. I served in the United States Army, including a deployment to a combat zone. I was prepared to fight. I'm grateful I didn't have to.

That service gave me reason to think seriously about what America actually is — not just as a place, but as an idea. The willingness to put yourself in harm's way for something clarifies what you believe about it. The principles and culture upon which this republic was founded are, in my view, worth that commitment. They have been proven so, many times over, by people who paid a far higher price than I did.

What I've found troubling — for a long time — is how many Americans have lost the thread. Not through malice, but through distance. The founding era feels remote, academic, and irrelevant to people who are living busy lives and have no particular reason to dig into 18th century political theory.

Rampart exists to close that distance. To make the Founders' actual words — not summaries, not interpretations, not talking points — accessible to the person who would genuinely engage with them if someone just made it easy. To show that the questions Madison, Hamilton, and Jefferson were wrestling with are the same questions we're wrestling with today. To pick up where the tavern left off.

I'm not a historian or a scholar. I'm an American who believes this republic is worth understanding, worth defending, and worth passing on — and who built a room for the conversation.

The mission

The rampart is a wall you stand on.

A rampart is a defensive fortification — the structure you stand on to protect what's inside. Madison called a free press "one of the great ramparts of liberty." Francis Scott Key watched through the night from a British ship, not knowing if Fort McHenry had held until dawn revealed the flag still flying over the walls.

The name is intentional. An informed citizenry is the rampart. Knowledge is the fortification. The republic doesn't defend itself — it requires people who understand what it is, why it matters, and what it costs to keep.

Rampart is for the person who wants to stand on that wall. Not to fight — but to understand. Because you can't defend what you don't know.

The weight of self-governance

Nobody is coming to save it.

The Founders knew they weren't just building a country. They were conducting an experiment the entire world was watching — and they said so explicitly. Jefferson called it "the world's best hope." Washington understood that a republic that actually worked was the most powerful argument for human freedom anywhere on earth.

That experiment is still running. And here's the uncomfortable truth that comes with self-governance: nobody is coming to save it.

Not a king, not a savior, not another nation waiting in the wings to pick up the pieces if this one fails. America is, in a very real sense, the last backstop. The republic that works — that has worked, imperfectly but persistently, for 250 years — is the reason so much of the world conducts trade, keeps peace, and carries on ordinary life with a degree of stability that would have been unimaginable to most of human history.

If it fails, there is no replacement waiting. There is no other country to pick up where this one left off.

That's not a reason for despair. It's a reason to understand what you have. To know what the Founders built, why they built it, and what it actually requires to keep. The Minutemen understood this. They didn't wait for someone else to stand on the wall.

"The preservation of the sacred fire of liberty and the destiny of the republican model of government are justly considered, perhaps, as deeply, as finally, staked on the experiment entrusted to the hands of the American people."
— George Washington, First Inaugural Address, 1789

Who this is for

Not scholars. Not activists. Everyone else.

Rampart is not an academic resource. It's not a political tool. It's not for people who already have bookshelves full of founding era history — though they're welcome here too.

It's for the person who vaguely remembers the Declaration from high school but couldn't tell you what comes after "all men are created equal." It's for the veteran who fought for these principles and wants to understand them more deeply. It's for the parent who wants to give their kids something real. It's for the skeptic who thinks the Founders were just wealthy elites protecting their interests — and who might be surprised by what the primary sources actually say.

It's for anyone willing to pull up a chair. The tavern is open.
